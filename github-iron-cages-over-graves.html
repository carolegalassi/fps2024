Iron Cages Over Graves: History, Purpose, and Legacy

Iron cages over graves, often called mortsafes, are a striking and mysterious sight in some historic cemeteries. These heavy metal structures, once common in parts of the United Kingdom and beyond, have sparked curiosity and speculation for generations. Were they meant to protect the dead, deter grave robbers, or serve as a symbol of status? This page explores the origins, construction, and cultural significance of iron cages over graves, drawing from historical sources, expert commentary, and multimedia resources.

What Are Iron Cages Over Graves?

Iron cages, or mortsafes, were robust metal frameworks placed over graves in the 18th and 19th centuries. Their primary purpose was to prevent the theft of recently buried bodies, a crime that became widespread due to the demand for cadavers in medical research and anatomy schools. In an era before modern legal protections, families and communities took extraordinary measures to safeguard their loved ones' remains.

The cages were typically constructed from wrought iron and anchored deep into the ground. Some were simple grids, while others featured intricate designs and heavy locking mechanisms. The presence of a mortsafe was a visible deterrent, signaling that a grave was protected against would-be body snatchers.

Historical Context and Use

Body snatching became a lucrative trade in the 18th and early 19th centuries, especially in Britain and Scotland. Medical schools needed fresh bodies for dissection, but legal supply was limited. This led to the rise of so-called resurrectionists, who exhumed corpses under cover of darkness. Communities responded by installing mortsafes, hiring guards, and even building watch houses in cemeteries.

Symbolism and Modern Interpretations

Today, iron cages over graves are rare but enduring symbols of a unique period in burial history. Some interpret them as a testament to the lengths families would go to protect their dead, while others see them as reminders of the uneasy relationship between science and society. In some cases, the cages have become local landmarks, drawing visitors interested in the macabre and the mysterious.
